# Speaker script (3 minutes, about 180 s)

## Slide 1

SLIDE 1 (about 60 seconds). A camera fixes the focus when you take the picture: one distance is sharp, everything else is blurred, and blur throws information away. So refocusing afterwards from a single photo is ill-posed, meaning there is no single right answer: many sharp scenes would give the same blurry picture. That is why we use a generative model. Our task: one photo at a known focus position goes in, all nine focus positions come out, which is called a focal stack, and then you click a pixel and we show the frame that is sharpest there. How we trained: we used the dataset from the original paper, 1,474 training stacks. We start from pretrained Stable Video Diffusion and update only its temporal transformer blocks, about 400 million of the 1.5 billion weights in its denoising network, the U-Net. Each step picks a random input frame, adds noise to the nine encoded frames and minimises the mean squared denoising error. We ran 4,000 steps for each of three variants on eight NVIDIA A100 graphics cards: 3.4 hours, about 54 dollars. In Blender we rendered scenes with true lens blur, so we know the right answer: the authors' released model produces a believable near-to-far sweep, but its sharpest frame lands one to three positions off, and the error depends on which frame we give it.

## Slide 2

SLIDE 2 (about 70 seconds). This is the architecture. It comes from Tedla and colleagues, SIGGRAPH Asia 2025, and it is built on Stable Video Diffusion; the parameter counts are our own measurements, in millions. Blue is pretrained and frozen, orange is the part we trained. The photo goes two ways. The variational autoencoder, or VAE, encodes it into a 4-channel latent image at one eighth of the size. CLIP, which stands for Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training, is a vision transformer that turns the photo into a single token of 1,024 numbers. The latent is placed in slot k of nine with zeros elsewhere, so the slot tells the network the input's focus position. It is joined with nine noisy latents, giving 8 channels into the denoising network, the U-Net. The U-Net has 1.5 billion weights. We trained only its 16 temporal transformer blocks: 398 million weights, 26 percent. Those blocks are attention along the frame axis, where each location looks at itself in the other frames. That is exactly what has to change when the frame axis means focus instead of motion. After 25 denoising steps the frozen decoder returns nine frames. Why was video the easiest route? A focal stack already is a video: the scene stays fixed and one thing changes smoothly. The pretrained model brings realistic images and consistent frames, we changed no architecture, and training from scratch was far beyond our 100 dollar budget.

## Slide 3

SLIDE 3 (about 50 seconds). This is the demo, recorded from our own viewer. Step one: a single photo goes in, focused at position 4 on a scale from 0, nearest, to 8, farthest. Step two: the model generates the whole focal stack, nine frames from near to far. Step three: you click a pixel, the viewer measures how sharp that spot is in each of the nine frames, and it shows the sharpest one, so whatever you clicked comes into focus. Click on the near black rook and frame 2 comes up. Click on the far white piece and frame 6 comes up. Every frame in this recording was generated by our fine-tuned model (variant A, 4,000 training steps), not by the authors' released model. For reference, on 20 held-out scenes the model shown here scores a peak signal-to-noise ratio of 28.5 decibels, against 31.1 for the authors' fully trained model, so it is a little softer, but it refocuses. This builds on Tedla and colleagues, SIGGRAPH Asia 2025. Thank you.
